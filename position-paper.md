<p>The Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus is the body of <strong>published works</strong> with at least one author affiliated with a Dutch research institution. Libraries do not look after everything that science produces; their focus is the literature, which is why we speak of <em>scholarly communication</em>.</p>
<p><strong>It includes</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>traditional publications, such as journal articles, books and book chapters, conference papers and theses;</li>
<li>non-traditional research outputs;</li>
<li>the different versions of a work that the Publish, Review, Curate (PRC) model distinguishes, such as the preprint, its reviews and the curated version of record;</li>
<li>journals published by the institutions themselves.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>It excludes</strong> the digital objects of other research entities, such as datasets, software, projects, persons, organisations, instruments and samples. These have their own domains and custodians.</p>
<p><strong>But it connects to them.</strong> Where it fits, the metadata of a published work must carry the identifiers of, and references to, these entities: the DOIs of underlying datasets and software, ORCID iDs, ROR IDs, project and grant identifiers. That places the literature in the context of the research endeavour, and makes it transparent and traceable what it is based on and who was involved.</p>
<h4 id="corpus-commons">The Corpus as a commons</h4>
<p>The Corpus is a shared resource, a commons. No single institution owns it. Every institution contributes to it and every institution depends on it. Like any commons, it only stays healthy when the community that uses it also takes care of it.</p>
<p>Organising the Corpus as a commons means that we agree together what good looks like, for example which metadata, identifiers and full texts a complete record of a Dutch published work should have. It means that we measure the state of the Corpus in the open, so that every institution can see where it is incomplete or inconsistent. And it means that each of us does their part at the source, in our own repository and CRIS, so that an improvement reaches everyone and not just a local copy.</p>
<p>The report <a href="https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21526773" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Organizing research as a commons</a> (Waegemaekers et al., 2026) explains how the idea of the commons can help to organise a fragmented research ecosystem, so that infrastructure, data and scholarly output are seen as a shared responsibility, and funding, support and governance are organised through collective action.</p>
<h4 id="corpus-ostrom">Ostrom's design principles</h4>
<p>Elinor Ostrom studied commons that have lasted for generations and found eight design principles that they share (Ostrom, 1990). This is how they apply to the Corpus:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Clear boundaries.</strong> The definition above says which works belong to the Corpus and which do not, and the federation makes clear which institutions take part.</li>
<li><strong>Rules that fit local conditions.</strong> Shared agreements on metadata, identifiers and full texts work with the systems and workflows each institution already has.</li>
<li><strong>Collective choice.</strong> The libraries and institutions affected by these agreements make and change them together, through the federation's shared governance.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring.</strong> The state of the Corpus is measured in the open, so that all members can see how the shared resource is doing.</li>
<li><strong>Graduated sanctions.</strong> When an agreement is not met, the first response is a signal and support, with firmer steps only if a problem persists.</li>
<li><strong>Conflict resolution.</strong> Members have quick and accessible ways to settle disagreements among themselves.</li>
<li><strong>Recognition of the right to organise.</strong> The boards of the institutions and national bodies respect the federation's right to make its own arrangements.</li>
<li><strong>Nested enterprises.</strong> Each institution looks after its own part of the Corpus, the federation coordinates at national level, and the Corpus connects to international infrastructures such as OpenAIRE.</li>
</ol>

## The Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus as a shared resource

The Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus is a shared resource, a commons. No single institution owns it. Every institution contributes to it, through the work of its researchers and the records in its repository and CRIS, and every institution depends on it to find, assess and preserve its research.

A commons only stays healthy when the community that uses it also takes care of it. That means agreeing together what good looks like, measuring it in the open and each doing our part at the source. This paper sets out how we, as libraries, want to do that together.

The Corpus is not kept in one place. Each institution looks after its own part, in its own repository and CRIS, and shared services bring the parts together: they harvest and validate the records, enrich them, preserve them and make them findable. Together these form a federated ecosystem, shown in [the DURF information chain](#the-durf-information-chain) below. Taking care of the Corpus means taking care of that chain.

## Why / problem statement

Dutch science is among the most productive and influential in the world: its impact is 72% above the global average<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>, and Dutch research institutes publish over 127,000 articles a year<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>. Libraries have an essential role in managing, curating, disseminating and preserving that output, through the repositories and CRIS systems that Dutch universities and research institutes have been building since the early 2000s.

These distributed but loosely connected systems are still maturing. While progress has been rapid, it has been lacking in coherent coordination. The part of the Corpus that we can monitor today - 1.9 million publications from 1995 until 2025, registered in the repositories and CRIS systems of 18 institutions - shows that at least half of these records are missing crucial pieces of metadata and the full text that are required for robust availability and preservation. Complete metadata and full texts are what the Corpus needs to be considered a digitally autonomous and highly valuable asset.

Additional challenges now loom large. The rise of agentic AI offers both threats and opportunities to scholarly publications; one threat could be that access to publications can be blocked and held to ransom in negotiations, an opportunity could be that our **Dutch scholarly works can be well represented, under our own conditions**, in AI models by offering our high-quality Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus.

## Central Aim of this paper

**This position paper is initially a call for university libraries, KB and OpenAIRE to commit to working together to help develop and sustain a robust backbone infrastructure for Dutch scholarly output that is shared, mature and resilient.**  

**Mandate and Context:** This commitment will contribute to the vision of [NPOS2030](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7433767) signed by the [Dutch covenant parties in 2023](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/documenten/2023/03/29/open-science-nl-versnelt-transitie-naar-open-science), and the [UNL Open Science Agenda in 2023](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10362464), and to the [“academic freedom statement”](https://www.universiteitenvannederland.nl/en/current/news/statement-on-academic-freedom-the-rectors-of-the-dutch-universities-2025) in 2025 by the Dutch university rectors.

**Institutional autonomy challenges**: This backbone is based on the concept of a [federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_(information_technology)). It acknowledges the value of “institutional autonomy”; translating in that university libraries are of different sizes, serve varied communities and have access to different levels of technical skills. It acknowledges that universities use different systems to manage publications, which are all at different levels of sophistication. But at the same time this makes the creation of a shared information infrastructure a challenge.

**Building a Strong Federation:** Therefore, the immediate focus of DURF, and this position paper, lies on the information chain that is supported by the various repositories and CRIS's, and can do so independent of technical choices that are made about these systems. DURF will create shared approaches, standards and tools for: the policies (theme 1) concerning these systems: for metadata exchange (theme 2), for full text harvesting (theme 3), for dissemination (theme 5), for long-term preservation (theme 4), and for co-management of the Netherlands Research Portal (theme 6).

There are six themes in the DURF project:

- **Theme 1 - Governance:** Integrate systems under shared governance
- **Theme 2 - Metadata:** Enable enriched metadata feedback-loop via the OpenAIRE Graph
- **Theme 3 - Fulltext:** Increase full-text collection
- **Theme 4 - Archive:** Preserve output via the KB's e-Depot
- **Theme 5 - Discovery:** Maximise discoverability through multiple channels, such as OpenAlex, Google, Wikidata and EOSC nodes
- **Theme 6 - Portal:** Provide a Dutch Portal as the outlet for curated research output

These themes will be explained in more detail below.

## Roles, Responsibilities and Parties

Every box in the diagram above plays a **role** in the information chain: the repositories and CRIS's, the harvester, the graph, the broker, the portal, the e-Depot, the resolver, the publishers and the scientific indexes. Each role comes with a **responsibility** towards the rest of the chain, as the table below shows. But a role cannot sign a position paper, hold a meeting, or make a pledge - only a **party** can. Every role is taken on by a party, and it is that party which carries the responsibility described throughout this paper, and which can count on the other parties in return.

This is why we need to form a Dutch Repository Federation, where we can hold each other accountable. The **core parties** in this federation are: the 13 University Libraries (hereafter: **UBs**) and KB National Library (hereafter: **KB**), both [members of UKB](https://ukb.nl/en/about-ukb/participating-members/), and OpenAIRE AMKE, a non-profit (hereafter: **OpenAIRE**). 
The core parties administer the largest share of the content in the OpenAIRE Graph that is associated with Dutch research performing organisations as a whole, and therefore form the backbone of this federation.
The federation welcomes all other Dutch research performing organisations (RPOs) to join at their own pace. This starts with the cooperation partners in the DURF project: the UKB associate members (KNAW, OU), the University Medical Centres and the Universities of Applied Sciences. It equally welcomes [other Dutch RPOs](https://www.surf.nl/en/about/members-of-surf) that are not (yet) part of the project.

| Role | Responsibility | Party |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Publishing platforms | Supply full texts and openly licensed metadata, such as CC0<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>, to the repositories (publishers, preprint servers) | Parties outside DURF's own governance |
| Institutional repository / CRIS | Capture and hold an institution's own research output and its metadata | Each university library |
| Metadata harvester & validator (OpenAIRE PROVIDE) | Collect and check records from repositories and CRIS's | OpenAIRE |
| OpenAIRE Graph | Aggregate, deduplicate and cross-reference records nationally and internationally | OpenAIRE |
| Enrichment (OpenAIRE Broker) | Return quality and enrichment suggestions to the source system | OpenAIRE |
| Netherlands Research Portal (OpenAIRE Connect) | Offer a curated, national outlet for Dutch scholarly output | OpenAIRE, with editorial and functional management by the DURF partners |
| e-Depot | Preserve deposited files for the long term | KB (National Library of the Netherlands) |
| URN:NBN resolver | Keep preserved files reliably citable and linkable | KB |
| Other research indexes (OpenAlex, Google, Wikidata, EOSC nodes) | Offer external discovery channels | Parties outside DURF's own governance |

The OpenAIRE Graph plays a central role in this chain. For the metadata of Dutch scholarly output, the OpenAIRE Graph:

- harvests the metadata from every Dutch repository and CRIS;
- unites and deduplicates it with what it knows from other sources;
- serves it out for specific uses: the Netherlands Research Portal, the KB e-Depot and, through the e-Depot, the URN:NBN resolver.

The full texts and the authoritative records stay with the institutions. This shows where DURF's influence lies: not in the part that OpenAIRE runs, but in what the institutions deliver into the Graph, and in what OpenAIRE delivers out of it.

Bringing these parties together under one set of shared rules is called **federated governance**: rather than one central authority controlling every system, each party - a university library, OpenAIRE, the KB - agrees to a small set of common rules (a shared metadata standard, diversity of licence information that allows fair use and ‘open, but closed if necessary’, clear interfaces between systems, how disagreements get resolved, etc. ), and is then responsible for meeting them within the system it operates. That is the shared governance that the DURF project, under theme 1 below, asks signees to commit to.

### Roles, Responsibilities and Reciprocity

Being part of the federation is not only a duty to the whole. It rests on reciprocity between the parties operating each system: each takes on specific responsibilities, and can expect something in return. The KB's e-Depot, for example, takes on the *responsibility* to archive and safely back up the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus deposited by university repositories - but it can only do so if it can *expect* to receive that metadata and full text from those repositories, in a usable, agreed form. Take either one away, and the other cannot be honoured.

[Appendix B](#b-roles-and-responsibilities-matrix) sets these roles and responsibilities out for every pair of parties in the chain. This position paper is not itself a legal agreement. How they relate to existing agreements, such as those between the KB and the institutions, will be worked out in the formal agreements under theme 1.

# The six themes

Below, for each theme, we describe what DURF aims to achieve and what it asks of the signees.

## 1. Integrate systems under shared governance

Bringing Dutch repositories, CRIS's, the harvester, the graph, the portal and the e-Depot together is bigger than agreeing a metadata standard - that belongs mostly under theme 2, below. This theme is about organising DURF as a research commons: metadata, full text and the infrastructure that carries them become resources that the signing parties manage, maintain and are accountable for together, as a community, rather than separately.

That takes more than good intentions. It takes the right legal structures - to share all fields of metadata openly, to capture and share full text, to jointly maintain and financially uphold the Netherlands Research Portal and OpenAIRE's services, and to commit to backing up content in the KB's e-Depot. Sharing openly here means the metadata, under an open licence such as CC0, so it can be reused without restriction. The full texts are a different matter: those are made open access under licences such as CC BY through the open access arrangements in the read and publish deals, which are outside the scope of DURF. These commitments apply both during the DURF project and after it ends. It is these commitments, brought together across all six themes, that this position paper asks its signees to make.

### Commitments for Governance

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs and KB believe they are the custodians of the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus (metadata and full texts), and are committed to the same **central aim** stated above.

*TACTICAL:*

* Dutch University Libraries (UBs) and KB become member of OpenAIRE AMKE (non-profit), and organise themselves in a National Membership Consortium (NaMeCo), giving Dutch institutions formal representation within OpenAIRE;

*OPERATIONAL:*

* We draft and finalise formal agreements (“afspraken stelsel”) on roles and responsibilities  between the parties that operate the systems they maintain to keep the federated infrastructure operational;
* UBs draft a legal structure (model contract for publisher negotiations)  under which all fields of metadata are shared under an open licence such as CC0, so they can move freely between systems;
* UBs and KB create a governance framework covering all six themes, reviewed toward the end of the project to keep it sustainable beyond it;
* UBs and KB jointly fund the shared infrastructure - contributing to the subscription licences that cover OpenAIRE's operating costs, and costs for functional management of the portal.
* We meet annually, at “DURF Federation Day” a National Symposium and General Assembly, to hold each other to these commitments.


## 2. Enable enriched metadata feedback via the OpenAIRE Graph

Validated, high-quality metadata is a condition for a successful, trusted repository. Poor metadata can mean broken links or context-free content. It erodes trust, one of the most important values that university repositories should embody.

The metadata in Dutch repositories and CRIS systems is used for strategic purposes within the Netherlands. Because the institutions register and check it themselves, it can be the most trusted source on Dutch scholarly output, also for third parties building new technology for finding and using research information - but only when its quality is consistently high.

For research indexes that quality is decisive. Only with consistent, complete metadata can they merge the Dutch additions, such as the full text in the repository and a clear affiliation to the institution, into their research graphs through deduplication. Today that affiliation path is largely missing: like ORCID iDs, ROR IDs are not present in the harvested records at all (see the [key figures](#why-problem-statement)). Openly licensed metadata (such as CC0) of consistent quality also allows new indexes and AI tools to be built on top of the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus without legal or financial barriers, opening the way for open alternatives to proprietary databases such as Scopus and Web of Science.

Rich metadata and complete coverage matter for research assessment as well. For this we foresee working with the OpenAIRE Broker and SURF's BROCCOLI project to enrich records in a validated chain. And high-quality metadata is the prerequisite for seeing what is still missing: which publications are absent from our repositories, and which full texts (theme 3).

A metadata harvester and validator (OpenAIRE PROVIDE) collects records from institutional repositories and CRIS systems into the OpenAIRE Graph, where they are deduplicated, cross-referenced and enriched (OpenAIRE Broker) - with the results fed back to the originating repositories rather than disappearing into a one-way pipeline. Those results are suggestions, not changes: each institution decides which enrichments it accepts into its own system. Aggregation and dissemination of metadata and the related content also becomes much easier if each institute registering research output does so in a unified manner.

### Commitments for Metadata

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs believe the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus is potentially a highly valuable asset when it has high quality metadata. 

*TACTICAL:*

* UBs make the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus available for harvest and quality enrichment to the OpenAIRE Graph, so Dutch research is connected to the European Open Science Cloud Nodes.

*OPERATIONAL:*

* UBs and KB form a NL Research Information Content Board (formerly known as ‘WISH’), through EduStandaard.nl, to steward the new shared metadata standard;
* UBs will contribute to develop an updated metadata application profile, translating the current NL-DIDL-MODS requirements to the OpenAIRE Guidelines for CRIS Managers (CERIF) and the OpenAIRE Guidelines for Literature Repository Managers (based on the DataCite metadata schema);
* UBs support each other in adopting that new profile, and monitor compliance;
* UBs will monitor the completeness of core persistent identifiers (ROR, ORCID, DOI) within their records, improve it, and make sure these identifiers are included in what they expose for harvesting;
* UBs will test the OpenAIRE’s metadata enrichment feedback-loop to improve completeness, and OpenAIRE will use the evaluation of the test to improve the graph enrichment algorithms.


## 3. Increase full-text collection

Repositories are not just collections of metadata. The collection of the actual research output (whether article, conference paper, book chapter or other output) is also part of the repository's function. Collecting the full text serves various purposes:

* it ensures that the digital object itself, not just its description, is held in public hands, underpinning the university's mission to preserve its intellectual output in the long term
* it allows for the upload and sharing of Green OA articles (particularly under NL's Taverne Agreement)
* it helps create corpora for use in text and data mining
* it provides a corpus for multiple AI uses, under controlled conditions that we can define ourselves
* it facilitates better aggregation and dissemination

Despite this, there are various challenges to importing full-text of research output - different tools and methodologies (with varying levels of effectiveness). Too many article PDFs are still missing from repositories, and this can be much better. Capturing full texts also raises legal questions - copyright, licences, embargoes, conditions for AI use and the terms of publisher agreements - which DURF will address explicitly. Of all themes, this one is likely to ask the most local effort from the libraries.

### Commitments for Full-texts

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs and KB believe they are the custodians of the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus, and feel responsible for the availability (findable and accessible) of metadata and full texts of the Dutch Research Literature.

*TACTICAL:*

* UBs will add full text to ‘empty’ metadata records, within a defined scope, from the past and present. 

*OPERATIONAL:*

* for past records the UBs come to an agreement on the scope and financial business case of the full-texts completeness of the back-log/back-files to aim for. (e.g only journal articles, closed and open, from that last 5 years, from all or specific publishers.) 
* for new records the UBs come to an agreement with publishers and other venues, in the read and publish deals, to deliver the rich metadata and a copy of the full text - preferably through a national solution, such as a router or switchboard, that delivers to every repository through a standard protocol such as SWORD, instead of separate arrangements per institution;
* UBs will jointly address the legal aspects of capturing and sharing full texts: copyright, licences, conditions for AI use, Taverne and the terms of publisher agreements;
* UBs will share and teach each other the tools, methods and services to meet these targets within the agreed scope.


## 4. Preserve output via the KB's e-Depot

While university libraries have capacity to manage their research outputs, tasks related to the long term  preservation of digital publications are undertaken by the National Library of the Netherlands (KB). The KB's e-Depot service ensures that files remain accessible in the long term, that they are associated with the right metadata, and - in the event of any digital disaster - that there is also a safe copy of the deposited material. A persistent URN:NBN resolver, also maintained by the KB, keeps preserved files reliably citable and linkable over time. This gives the KB a unique role in relation to university repositories. For unique content, such as journals published by the institutions themselves, a preserved copy at the national library is of great added value: the KB is registered as an archiving agency in the Keepers Registry, and preservation with such an agency is something a journal needs to show, for example for the DOAJ Seal.

### Commitments for Preservation

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs and KB believe that the Dutch Scholarly Communication Corpus is a highly valuable asset for generations to come, and must be preserved and be made accessible for the long term.

*OPERATIONAL:*

* The UBs will share their repository outputs with the e-Depot for the purposes of long-term preservation.
* The KB will preserve and resolve the metadata and full text that is delivered by the UBs.
* The KB will assign a URN:NBN to each preserved publication and keep it resolving to the current, preserved location;
* The UBs will record that URN:NBN in their repository records, so it travels with the metadata.


## 5. Maximise discoverability through multiple channels

Good metadata is only useful if it reaches researchers where they actually search. Dutch scholarly output should be discoverable not only through institutional repositories, but through the channels researchers actually use: aggregators such as OpenAlex, general search engines such as Google (Scholar), open knowledge bases such as Wikidata, and the network of EOSC nodes that connects European research infrastructure. Wikidata is a particularly strong candidate: it is open, widely reused by other services, and one of the sources used to train AI. 

Discoverability is no longer only for human consumption. We want all publicly funded Dutch research to be open for humans and machines alike. This is of strategic importance. AI models, wherever in the world they are built, increasingly shape how people find and understand knowledge. 
We believe that Dutch research should be well represented in them, so that the value of Dutch science, and its careful search for what is true, stays present in those models and in the answers they give. That openness needs to happen with the right conditions: lawfully and on fair terms.

### Commitments for Discoverability

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs believe Dutch scholarly output must be findable and accessible for researchers and the public on a multitude of relevant outlets and venues to maximise discovery.


*OPERATIONAL:*

* UBs will share and teach each other the tools and methods to maximise the discoverability of Dutch scholarly output, for human consumption and machines alike. 


## 6. Provide a Dutch Portal as the outlet for curated research output

Alongside the many channels through which Dutch scholarly output can be discovered, DURF partners also commit to the Netherlands Research Portal (built on OpenAIRE Connect) as the national, curated showcase of that output - a single outlet where the results of shared governance, enriched metadata and full-text collection come together for a Dutch and international audience.

A portal is only as good as the care it gets. The DURF partners therefore do more than deliver their output to it: they share the responsibility for running it, through its editorial care and functional management, and for developing it further. The signees provide the input for new functionality, and so decide together where the portal goes next. If they want to take it in the direction of a public portal, that is possible. The KB's WijsNL project shows what that could look like: a search environment that makes finding and understanding research possible for everyone, starting with the open access publications of researchers at Dutch universities - the very output that DURF brings together. The aim is a portal that serves not only libraries, but everyone who wants clear, understandable access to Dutch research and to the researchers behind it: researchers, policy makers, professionals and citizens looking for knowledge or expertise.

### Commitments for Portal

Therefore, the signees of the DURF Position Paper agree the following:

*STRATEGIC:*

* UBs and KB believe the Netherlands Research Portal should be the prime public-facing outlet of Dutch research, researchers and research projects; so that researchers, policy makers, professionals and citizens can find and understand Dutch research and the experts behind it;

*OPERATIONAL:*

* UBs share their curated research output with the Netherlands Research Portal, and keep it up to date;
* UBs and KB take shared and financial responsibility for the portal's editorial care, functional management;
* UBs and KB take shared and financial responsibility for the technical hosting and maintenance of the portal by OpenAIRE;
* UBs and KB provide input for new functionality and help develop the portal further;
* OpenAIRE will provide a development roadmap for the Connect Portal implementation.

## B. Roles and responsibilities matrix

Each filled cell reads as a sentence. The small label at the top says who it is **from** (the row) and **to** (the column): the role in the row takes on that responsibility, and the role in the column can expect it in return. Blank cells mean no direct relationship in this chain. If any one party cannot commit to its cell, the balance in that relationship - and often others downstream of it - needs to be revisited.

Two examples:

- **Repository &rarr; e-Depot**: the repository supplies its full text and metadata for archiving, and the e-Depot can expect to receive them.
- **e-Depot &rarr; Repository**: the e-Depot shows the repository that this content is safely backed up and archived, so the repository can count on it being kept safe.

Together, these two cells, mirrored across the diagonal, show the reciprocity: each side takes something on, and each side gets something back. Blue cells deliver something onward along the chain; purple cells return something to an earlier link.

The matrix is an illustration of the relationships in the chain, not a legal agreement. How it relates to existing agreements, such as those between the KB and the institutions, will be worked out under theme 1.

## C. Technical background: data products, data lake and data contracts

The approach in this paper borrows a few concepts from [data mesh architecture](https://www.datamesh-architecture.com/), which SURF's Open Research Information programme is also exploring for the ORI DuckLake.

- **Data product.** A bundle of a pipeline, the data it produces and a description of what it offers, with an owner who is responsible for it. Applied here: each system in the chain is a shared building block, but the commitments belong to the party operating it.
- **Data lake.** A place where data is refined in layers, often called bronze, silver and gold. DURF builds no lake of its own, but the OpenAIRE Graph plays that role for the metadata: it harvests the metadata as delivered (bronze), unites and deduplicates it (silver), and serves it out for specific uses such as the Netherlands Research Portal, the e-Depot and the resolver (gold). Because the full texts and authoritative records stay with the institutions, the Graph can always be rebuilt from the sources.
- **Data contracts.** The handoffs between parties can be written down as data contracts: machine-readable agreements on what is delivered, in which form and of which quality. Examples exist for every arrow in the diagram, including a Dutch application profile of the OpenAIRE Guidelines for CRIS Managers (see [Read more](#d-read-more)).

The Dutch slice of the OpenAIRE Graph is available as the `netherlands` community, for example through the Graph API: `https://api.openaire.eu/graph/v2/researchProducts?relCommunityId=netherlands&type=publication`.
